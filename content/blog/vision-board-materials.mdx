You can spend forty-five minutes choosing the right posterboard color or ten minutes making a board that works. Most of the guides online have a reason to make this seem complicated. The supplies section of a vision board tutorial is not where the complexity lives.

Here's the actual list, what each choice affects, and the single decision that determines whether any of this does anything.

## The short materials list

**The base:**

- Posterboard (22"×28" is standard; it's cheap and easy to hang)
- Cork board (more durable, you can use push pins instead of glue — good if you like rotating images)
- Canvas board or wooden panel (heavier, harder to move, but doesn't curl)

Any of these work. The choice doesn't affect the outcome.

**Images:**

- Old magazines — interior design, travel, lifestyle, and fashion publications tend to have usable imagery across most goal categories
- Printed photos (your own or ones you print from the internet)
- Printed text — words, phrases, short statements cut and pasted

**Adhesives:**

- Glue stick (easiest, works on paper images)
- Mod Podge (if you want a sealed, glossy finish that lasts — overkill for most people)
- Double-sided tape (cleaner-looking, no curl)
- Push pins (for cork boards)

**Optional:**

- Scissors or a paper cutter
- Markers or pens for writing directly on the board
- Washi tape, stickers, stamps — decorative elements if that process is meaningful to you

That's the complete list. Everything else is preference.

## Physical vs digital

A digital vision board — a Pinterest board, a saved folder, a dedicated app — works if you actually open it regularly. The mechanism is the same as a physical board: you need to look at it. The question is which format makes that more likely for you specifically.

Physical boards have one advantage: they exist in your space. A corkboard by the mirror doesn't require you to navigate to it. It's there.

Digital boards have one advantage: you can refine them without glue and scissors, and access them anywhere.

Neither is inherently better. The [psychology of when vision boards work](/blog/vision-boards-for-manifesting) has nothing to do with paper versus screen and everything to do with whether you return to it consistently.

If you're not sure, start physical. You can always go digital later. The reverse is harder to motivate.

## The craft trap

Here's the failure mode that's specific to vision boards as a craft project: people spend three hours on design and zero time using the result.

The decorating — carefully trimming images, color-coordinating quadrants, hand-lettering your intentions — is enjoyable and feels productive. It is not the practice. It is the preparation for the practice.

A vision board that took twenty minutes to make but gets thirty seconds of your attention each morning is doing more than an elaborate collage that lives face-down on a shelf.

This is the reason most vision board tutorials spend so much time on materials and aesthetics: those are the parts that make for good content. The unglamorous instruction — *put it somewhere you'll actually see it and look at it briefly every day* — doesn't expand into a list of Amazon supplies.

## The one choice that actually matters

**Where you put it.**

A vision board that isn't visible is a scrapbook. The location is not a detail — it's the mechanism. Where will you be standing or sitting when you want to use it?

Options that tend to work:

- Bathroom mirror or above the sink (you're there every morning)
- The wall facing your desk (background attention throughout the day)
- Beside your bed (visible from where you wake up)
- Phone lock screen or desktop wallpaper (if you're going digital)

Options that don't work: inside a wardrobe, stacked against a wall, on a shelf behind other things.

Once it's placed well, the [practice of looking at it takes thirty seconds](/blog/30-second-daily-ritual). Not a meditation session. Not an affirmation ritual. A glance, with presence. That's what it's actually for.

## When you're skeptical about vision boards

If the whole thing feels a bit much, [manifestation without vision boards](/blog/manifestation-without-vision-boards) is a reasonable alternative — the same underlying practice of regularly placing your attention on what you want, without the corkboard. The materials aren't the point. The attention is.

The supplies are just the cost of admission. What you do after you make it is the part that counts.
