The mirror pep talk. The bathroom stall before the panel starts. "I am confident, I am capable" — repeated three times, with increasing desperation.

Most people have done some version of this. Most have also noticed it doesn't quite work.

The good news: self-talk before interviews isn't useless. The research actually supports it — but not the version that sounds like a motivational poster. What works is more specific, and more honest.

## When "I am confident" makes things worse

A 2009 study by psychologist Joanne Wood found that for people with low self-esteem, repeating positive self-statements made mood worse, not better. The mechanism is simple: when you assert something that contradicts your felt experience, your brain pushes back. The gap between the statement and reality makes you more aware of the gap, not less.

In an interview context, this compounds. "I am going to nail this" right before a panel adds outcome-pressure you can't control. "I am calm" when you're clearly not becomes another thing to fail at. You end up monitoring whether the affirmation is taking hold — which is more mental activity on top of an already-overloaded system.

This is the same reason [why affirmations don't feel like yours](/blog/affirmations-that-dont-feel-fake) when they're too aspirational. The bigger the gap between what you're saying and what you actually feel, the worse it tends to go. That gap is the mechanism worth understanding, not working around. If your anxiety tends to run high before evaluative situations, the [affirmations for anxiety](/blog/affirmations-for-anxiety) piece covers the same backfire mechanism in more depth.

## What the research actually supports

Three specific types of self-talk show up in credible studies. None look like what's on the wellness content rack.

**Values-based affirmation.** Research by J. David Creswell at Carnegie Mellon found that reflecting on your core personal values — not your personality traits — measurably reduces the cortisol response to stress. This works in high-stakes situations including academic tests and evaluative situations. The mechanism is attention: instead of the threat dominating your focus, a value you care about gives your attention something real to orient toward.

Before an interview, this might sound like: "I care about doing work I'm proud of. That's why I'm here."

**Third-person self-talk.** Research by Ethan Kross and colleagues found that using your own name creates psychological distance — a small but reliable shift from "I am a disaster" to "Sarah, you've handled harder things." That distance reduces anxiety and improves performance under pressure. It sounds odd. It works.

**The "Will I?" framing.** A 2010 study in *Psychological Science* found that framing self-talk as a question — "Will I stay calm?" — is associated with better outcomes than declarative statements — "I will stay calm." Questions activate intrinsic motivation. Statements create pressure to live up to a claim.

None of these ask you to believe something untrue. That's the distinction.

## Five phrases worth saying

These aren't magic. They're reminders — of things you already know, already have done, or already hold. The difference between a reminder and a convincer is whether you'd believe the sentence on a Tuesday when nothing was at stake. If yes, it's a reminder. If you'd need to build up to it, it's a convincer — and convincers backfire under pressure.

**"I've prepared for this. I know my work."**
Specific and verifiable. You did prepare. You do know your work. This isn't an aspiration — it's a fact, if the preparation happened. Anchor to that.

**"I'm here to have a real conversation, not to perform."**
Reframes the threat. The best interviews are genuinely conversational. This shifts you from performance-mode (hypervigilant, self-conscious) toward curiosity-mode. Different affect, different presence.

**"I've figured out harder things than this."**
Draw on a specific memory if you can — something you navigated when you didn't know you could. The specificity is what makes it credible to your nervous system.

**"I'm curious about them too."**
Shifts the frame from "I am being evaluated" to "we are evaluating each other." Both are true. Which one you're inside of shapes how you carry yourself in the room.

**"What would [your name] do here?"**
Third-person framing, useful mid-interview when your mind goes blank. Creates a sliver of distance between the anxious "I" and the competent version of yourself you're trying to access. Reach for it when a question lands hard and you need a second.

## The right moment to use them

The research on performance anxiety suggests that rumination in the long window before a high-stakes event amplifies worry rather than reducing it. The effective window for values-based self-talk appears to be 10–15 minutes before, not two hours before.

So: not while lying awake at midnight. Not obsessively on the commute. Once, deliberately, in the minutes before you walk in. Then let it go.

If pre-interview routines have ever left you more anxious than when you started — because you spent an hour doing breathing exercises and journaling while the fear just grew — that's the two-hour problem. [A short morning ritual](/blog/morning-affirmations) is worth more than a long anxious one.

## What affirmations can't do

If these phrases feel insufficient, they might be. The research on interview performance consistently finds that behavioral preparation — knowing your answers, having stories ready, practicing out loud — is the strongest predictor of both confidence and performance. Affirmations don't substitute for preparation. They're a frame for the preparation you've already done.

If you're still in the job search phase, [affirmations for finding a new job](/blog/new-job-affirmations) cover a different set of concerns — the longer, slower anxiety of not yet having an offer, which calls for different language.

The useful question before any high-stakes moment isn't "what can I say to feel ready?" It's "what do I already have that's true?"

Anchor there. The rest is noise.
