In December 2022, a TikTok creator named Laura Galebe posted that she gets "the most insane opportunities" simply by expecting them. A few weeks later, two women in a car attributed their exam passes and housing wins to repeating "I'm so lucky, everything just works out for me." The hashtag #luckygirlsyndrome hit 150 million views by the end of 2023.

The honest question is whether any of that is real — or whether it's just a pretty way to do affirmations.

The answer is: both. And the part that's real is more interesting than the trend knows.

## What Richard Wiseman actually found

In the late 1990s and early 2000s, psychologist Richard Wiseman ran a formal [Luck Lab](https://www.stat.berkeley.edu/~aldous/Real-World/wiseman.html) at the University of Hertfordshire. He recruited 400 self-identified lucky and unlucky people and ran them through a series of experiments.

In one, participants were asked to count photos in a newspaper. On page 2 — right there, visible — was a half-page message: "STOP COUNTING — THERE ARE 43 PHOTOGRAPHS."

Lucky people noticed it. Unlucky people, who were hunched over and counting intently, missed it.

Wiseman's conclusion: people who describe themselves as lucky aren't more fortunate by chance. They carry a more relaxed, open attentional style that lets them *notice* opportunities others walk past. They talk to strangers. They follow up on hunches. They pay looser attention to the horizon.

Then he did the most interesting thing: he coached self-described "unlucky" people to adopt the same attentional habits. Nearly all reported significant improvements in their lives within a month.

Luck, it turns out, is partly a scanning behavior.

## What confirmation bias adds

Here's the other half: believing you're lucky primes your brain to register and *remember* lucky breaks you'd otherwise filter out as coincidence.

This is confirmation bias operating through selective attention. Once "things work out for me" becomes your frame, your brain selects for evidence. The bad stuff still happens — but you encode the good stuff more heavily, which creates a genuine subjective experience of things going your way, even when the objective ratio hasn't changed much.

This sounds like a debunking. It isn't. Changing what you notice, and what you remember, changes what you do next. You take the call. You email back. You stay in the room a little longer.

Confirmation bias here isn't a bug. It's the mechanism.

## Where lucky girl syndrome gets it wrong

The trend asks you to repeat a phrase. The science points to something behavioral: relaxed attention, openness to strangers, following through on low-probability hunches.

The phrase might help — if it genuinely loosens something. Martin Seligman's [learned optimism](https://www.psychologytoday.com/us/blog/beyond-school-walls/202409/practicing-learned-optimism) research shows positive expectancy drives persistence and action, not passive receiving. Optimistic salespeople in a landmark MetLife study sold 31% more than pessimistic peers. The mechanism was behavior change, not the universe.

But the [BBC Science Focus](https://www.sciencefocus.com/news/lucky-girl-syndrome-a-psychologist-explains-how-tiktoks-extreme-positive-thinking-trend-could-backfire) and [Psychology Today](https://www.psychologytoday.com/us/blog/social-instincts/202305/3-ways-the-lucky-girl-syndrome-may-be-hurting-you) both note the same failure modes:

**Magical thinking creep.** Repeating that you're lucky can slide into expecting outcomes without the behavior that actually produces them. Lucky girl syndrome strips out the behavioral mechanism — relaxed openness, action-taking, following hunches — and leaves only the affirmation.

**Suppressed reality.** Forcing positive narration when things are genuinely hard doesn't make you luckier. Research on toxic positivity consistently shows that suppressing negative emotion increases despair. Acknowledging that something went badly, then redirecting, performs better than performing optimism at problems.

**Structural blindness.** Race, income, health, and geography shape what "things working out" even means. [The Washington Post](https://www.washingtonpost.com/wellness/2023/01/20/lucky-girl-syndrome-tiktok/) noted what the trend itself cannot: there is zero peer-reviewed research on lucky girl syndrome as a practice. And when it doesn't work for someone, attributing that to insufficient belief is a quiet cruelty.

## The useful kernel

The useful version of lucky girl syndrome isn't a mantra. It's a retraining of attentional habits: less tunnel focus, more noticing, more following up. It's the [reticular activating system](/blog/reticular-activating-system-manifestation) doing exactly what it's built to do — filtering the world through your current priorities, good or bad.

If "I'm so lucky, everything works out for me" helps you walk into a room a little more open, a little less guarded, and notice the thing you'd have scrolled past, it's doing something real. If it's a performance you maintain against evidence, it's going to exhaust you.

[Half-belief](/blog/half-belief-is-the-honest-place-to-start) is where Wiseman's research quietly lands. You don't have to be convinced anything cosmic is working in your favor. You just have to stay open enough to notice what's already in front of you.

That's a daily practice. Not a mindset. Not a vibe. Thirty seconds a day of placing your attention on what you actually want, and then getting on with your Tuesday. That's [what manifestation actually does](/blog/does-manifestation-actually-work) when it works.
