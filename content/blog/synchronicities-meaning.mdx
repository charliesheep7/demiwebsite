You think of someone you haven't spoken to in two years. They text you an hour later. You've been weighing a decision about your career and then a stranger on the train says exactly the thing you needed to hear.

Most people have a story like this. The question is what to do with it.

## Jung named it first

Carl Jung coined "synchronicity" in the early twentieth century to describe meaningful coincidences — events that are not causally connected but feel deeply related. He defined it carefully: not just any coincidence, but one that carries *meaning* for the person who experiences it.

Jung's most famous example came from a therapy session. A patient was describing a dream in which she received a gold scarab beetle as a gift. At that exact moment, Jung heard a tapping at the window. He opened it and caught a rose chafer beetle — the closest thing to a scarab that exists in central Europe. He handed it to her. The timing, he argued, was meaningful in a way that causality couldn't explain.

His term for this was "acausal connecting principle" — a pattern linking inner experience and outer event that doesn't run through cause and effect. He was clear, and so were his critics, that this was not standard science.

## What science says instead

Most psychologists today would describe synchronicities through a different lens: confirmation bias and selective attention.

You think of someone frequently. Eventually you run into them, and the encounter feels uncanny — because you remember the thinking-of-them, not the hundred times you thought of them and nothing followed. The [reticular activating system](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Reticular_activating_system) — the part of the brainstem that filters what reaches conscious notice — gets tuned to whatever you've marked as significant. When you're in a heightened emotional state, weighing a big decision, grieving something, hoping for a sign, the filter widens and more coincidences get caught and remembered.

This isn't debunking. It's the actual mechanism that makes synchronicities useful: you notice more coincidences when you're paying attention. And [paying attention to what matters](/blog/attention-as-manifestation) is the practice.

## Concrete examples people actually encounter

Not the mystical kind. The ordinary kind:

- You've been thinking seriously about changing careers. In one day you overhear a conversation about someone who just quit to freelance, and you read an article about the same thing.
- You keep running into the same person in unrelated contexts. You finally introduce yourself.
- A phrase, a name, or an image shows up in three different places in a single day in a way that feels too specific to dismiss.

The question isn't whether these are cosmic signs. The question is whether noticing them points you somewhere useful.

## A practical exercise for one week

Keep a coincidence log. Not a spiritual journal — just a running note on your phone titled something boring like "weird thing today."

When something feels synchronistic, write down three things quickly:
1. What were you thinking about or hoping for?
2. What appeared?
3. What does it seem to be pointing at?

At the end of the week, look at the whole list. You'll notice a pattern — not in the universe, but in your own attention. The themes you keep circling. The questions that keep surfacing through different doors. That pattern is data. It tells you what you're actually focused on, underneath the daily noise.

This is how [a daily reflection practice](/blog/daily-manifestation-ritual) uses synchronicities usefully: not as external confirmation, but as a mirror for what you're already attending to.

## The limits worth naming

Synchronicities are not a reliable communication system. Assigning meaning to coincidences can also reinforce what you want to believe rather than what's true. A bad relationship can start to feel cosmically sanctioned if you're looking for signs that it's right.

A useful check: is this coincidence prompting action toward something genuinely good, or is it providing permission to avoid a harder choice? If the coincidence is being used as an excuse — to stay, to delay, to believe — it's doing the opposite of what's useful.

## How to fit this into an ordinary day

The mistake people make with synchronicities is treating them as a separate practice that requires deliberate spiritual effort. They work better as ambient notice-and-note.

- When something feels weirdly timed, spend ten seconds on why it caught your attention.
- Don't interpret immediately. Let it sit. Return to it at the end of the day.
- Connect it to what you've been thinking about honestly — not what you want it to mean.

This fits inside [the 30-second daily check-in](/blog/half-belief-is-the-honest-place-to-start) without requiring anything mystical. What have you been noticing? What's been showing up?
