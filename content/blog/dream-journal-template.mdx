The dream disappears as you reach for your phone. You can feel its shape — something about a house you've never lived in, someone from years ago — but by the time the coffee is made, it's gone.

This is the ordinary problem with dreams. They're available for about ninety seconds after waking, then mostly gone. A dream journal catches them. It doesn't require interpretation or deep symbolism work — just five fields on paper before you look at your screen.

Here's a template that fits in the margin of a real morning.

## The five-field template

The goal isn't completeness. It's capture.

**Date and time of waking** — optional, but useful for spotting patterns across weeks.

**What happened** — Two or three sentences. Not "I dreamed about my childhood home" but "I was standing in the kitchen of the house I grew up in and couldn't find the door." Scene, not summary.

**Who appeared** — Names or descriptions. Even background characters. Especially background characters.

**The emotional tone** — One word or a short phrase: anxious, light, confused, strangely calm. This is often more reliable than the narrative when recall is thin.

**One image that stayed** — The detail that's still with you an hour later. Write it exactly as it appears, without analysis.

Five fields. A practiced hand fills this in under two minutes. A slow morning, maybe four. The design is deliberately low-effort because [practices that require a clear desk and twenty minutes don't survive ordinary Tuesdays](/blog/daily-practice-that-sticks).

## What you're actually doing

Dream journaling sits somewhere between self-reflection and attention training. Two things it's genuinely doing — and one thing it probably isn't.

The first: emotion processing. Research by sleep scientist Antonio Zadra at the Université de Montréal suggests that emotional content in dreams correlates closely with psychological well-being — and that attending to that content consciously, even briefly, changes how it sits. Imagery Rehearsal Therapy, an evidence-based cognitive behavioral approach for nightmares, uses exactly this mechanism: record the dream, engage with it in waking consciousness, reduce its hold. You don't need to have nightmares for this to matter.

The second: pattern recognition. After a few weeks, recurring themes become visible — a particular location, a type of conflict, a person who keeps returning. Patterns you couldn't notice in the moment appear in sequence. This is [the same thing that happens with a written manifestation practice](/blog/how-to-manifest-something-on-paper): writing externalizes what the mind keeps private, and the externalized version is easier to read.

What it probably isn't: a clean window into unconscious meaning. Dream interpretation is an old and mostly speculative practice. Symbols don't carry consistent meaning across people, cultures, or days. The snake in last Tuesday's dream almost certainly doesn't mean anything specific. Approach the template as observation, not translation.

## How to use it without turning it into homework

The single failure mode is a template too demanding for a groggy weekday morning.

Keep the notebook on your nightstand, not across the room. The longer the gap between waking and writing, the less you have. Your phone doesn't count — the screen triggers notification-checking that displaces the dream before you've written anything down.

Write in whatever is legible. Speed matters more than penmanship here. Shorthand, fragments, a quick sketch if it captures something faster than words — all fine.

Don't interpret while you write. Note what happened; save the "I think this means" for later, if at all. The analytical mind that wants to make sense of things will rush past the raw material if you let it.

If you wake in the middle of the night with a vivid dream, write two lines then. Even "red elevator, felt trapped" is enough to reconstruct more in the morning.

## Fitting it into an ordinary morning

The template works best attached to an existing anchor — something you already do right when you wake. Before coffee. Before your phone. Before the day starts narrating itself at you.

This is the logic [behind any attention practice that survives daily life](/blog/attention-as-manifestation): the practice doesn't need to be long, but it does need a consistent cue. "Every morning when I open my journal" is a cue. "When I feel like it" isn't.

Five fields. Two minutes. Same time, same order. That's the full design.
