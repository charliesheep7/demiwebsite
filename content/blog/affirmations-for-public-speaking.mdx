About 75% of people have some fear of public speaking. You're not unusual for feeling it. What's unusual is knowing what to say to yourself about it — because almost every piece of common advice on this is wrong.

"I am calm." "I am confident." "I've got this." These are the phrases most people reach for before a presentation. They're also the ones most likely to make things worse.

## The counterintuitive finding

In 2014, a Harvard researcher named Alison Wood Brooks ran a controlled experiment. She had 140 people give a persuasive speech under pressure — recorded, judged, the whole thing. One group was told to say "I am excited" before speaking. Another was told to try to calm down. A third said nothing in particular.

The people who said "I am excited" were rated significantly more persuasive, more competent, and more relaxed than those who tried to calm down.

The mechanism is simple: anxiety and excitement are physiologically identical. Both are high-arousal states. Your heart rate is up, your hands might shake, your focus is narrowed. Telling yourself to be calm asks your nervous system to downshift entirely — which is a much harder physiological task than shifting valence. Reappraising "I'm nervous" as "I'm excited" works *with* the body's existing state, not against it.

You're not lying to yourself. You're describing the same physical reality from a different angle.

## Why "I am confident" tends to backfire

The standard affirmation formula — declarative, aspirational, first-person — fails for a specific reason. When you repeat a statement that contradicts your felt experience, your brain registers the contradiction. The gap between what you're claiming and what you actually feel becomes the most prominent thing in the room.

This is the same backfire pattern that shows up with [affirmations for anxiety](/blog/affirmations-for-anxiety): the bigger the gap between the assertion and reality, the more anxious you tend to become, not less. The statement becomes evidence of how badly you need it — and how little it's working.

Research by Ibrahim Senay and colleagues found a cleaner version: framing self-talk as a question ("Will I do well?") outperformed declarative statements ("I will do well"). Questions activate intrinsic motivation. Declarations add pressure you can't control. Before a speech, "Will I find the words?" is often more useful than "I will be brilliant."

The same logic explains [why affirmations don't feel like yours](/blog/affirmations-that-dont-feel-fake) when they're too far from where you actually are.

## What actually works

Three specific techniques have genuine research behind them.

**The excitement reframe.** Say "I'm excited" out loud, once, in the 60 seconds before you go on. That's it. Brooks found this was enough to shift measured performance outcomes. You don't need to mean it perfectly. The shift in framing does real work.

**Values affirmation.** Before a high-stakes presentation, take 10 minutes to write briefly about something you actually care about — not your skills, not your preparation, not your professional identity. Something that matters beyond this room: a relationship, a creative practice, something you believe in. Research by J. David Creswell found this measurably reduces the cortisol response to evaluative stress. The mechanism: when you remember what you value, the stakes of any single performance shrink to their actual size. You're no longer gambling your self-worth on one speech.

This isn't journaling as a stall. It's 10 minutes, and it works because it gives your nervous system a broader context.

**Third-person self-talk.** When your mind goes blank mid-presentation, or when spiraling thoughts show up in the hour before, use your own name. "Sam, you know this material." Research by Ethan Kross found that self-distancing through third-person framing reduces anxiety and post-performance rumination. It sounds strange. It creates enough psychological distance to access your own competence instead of your own fear.

## When to use each

Timing matters. The values-writing works best 10–15 minutes before — it's too slow for the final 60 seconds, but useful for the hour leading up. The excitement reframe belongs in the final minute. Third-person self-talk is available mid-performance, when you need to catch yourself and redirect.

Long anxious routines in the two hours before often amplify the very nerves they're designed to address. A [short morning ritual](/blog/morning-affirmations) that happens daily — building the habit of brief, deliberate attention before the day starts — is worth more than a panicked 45-minute prep session.

## The part affirmations can't fix

Preparation is still the main thing. Knowing your material, having practiced out loud, having stories ready — these are the strongest predictors of confidence and performance. Affirmations don't substitute for that. They're a frame for the preparation you've already done.

If pre-speech anxiety is persistent and severe enough to be avoidance-inducing, it's worth treating on its own terms, separate from any ritual. That's a different problem than garden-variety nerves.

For the majority of people with ordinary speech anxiety — the kind that shows up before a presentation but doesn't derail their life — the honest answer is: try "I'm excited" once and see what changes. It's not a cure. It's a reframe. That might be enough.
