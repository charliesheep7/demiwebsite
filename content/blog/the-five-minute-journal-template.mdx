The five-minute journal is a prompt loop, not a product. You can buy the official version — a physical journal published by Intelligent Change — or you can recreate the format in any notebook you already own. The prompts are the practice.

Here's the format, what it actually does, and how to adapt it if five minutes is too many.

## The template (morning + evening)

**Morning — 3 minutes**

1. Three things I'm grateful for today:
2. What would make today feel like a good day?
3. One thing I'm going to do (or be) today:

**Evening — 2 minutes**

1. Three things that happened today that I'm glad about:
2. One thing I could have done differently:
3. Did the morning's "one thing" happen?

That's it. Five minutes total, split across two small windows. No blank pages, no pressure to write paragraphs.

## Why morning + evening matters

The two-part structure is doing something specific. Morning journaling is [future-facing](/blog/future-self-journaling): you're priming your brain to notice what you said mattered. The intention prompt — "what would make today feel good?" — is a softer version of goal-setting, without the performance pressure of a to-do list.

Evening journaling is different. Looking back at three positive moments is a form of [gratitude practice](/blog/gratitude-journal-prompts), but it's tied to your actual day rather than abstract appreciation. The question about what you could have done differently isn't self-criticism — it's a feedback loop. You see the gap without having to shame yourself into changing it.

Together, they bookend the day on purpose. Intention in the morning, reflection at night.

## What it honestly does

The five-minute journal doesn't change your circumstances. What it does, done consistently:

- Trains the reticular activating system — the brain's filtering mechanism — to flag things that match what you said mattered that morning
- Builds a low-effort daily reflection habit, because the prompts remove the blank-page problem
- Creates a short paper trail of your actual days, which is more useful than memory for noticing patterns

It doesn't work as a substitute for therapy, a plan, or action. It's a two-minute morning scan and a two-minute evening review. That's the honest scope.

## A version for when five minutes is too long

If the morning-plus-evening format isn't surviving your actual week, try dropping the evening entirely. Just the three morning gratitudes and one intention. That's under two minutes.

[Daily reflection questions](/blog/daily-reflection-questions) work well if you want to expand the evening portion — specific prompts help when "three good things" starts to feel mechanical.

And if even two minutes is too much to commit on the ordinary Tuesday you don't want to open a notebook, [journal tracker ideas](/blog/journal-tracker-ideas) covers the minimal version — one-word mood, one intended action, done.

## How to adapt the format

The original prompts are a starting point. You can swap in:

- "Three things that went well" instead of gratitude (more concrete, less woo)
- "What do I want to pay attention to today?" instead of an intention
- "What surprised me?" instead of the "could have done differently" prompt

The only constraint worth keeping: **always pair something forward-looking with something backward-looking**. That's the structural reason the format works. One scans ahead, one looks back.

## What the format doesn't need

- A special notebook (any works)
- A particular time (morning is useful but not mandatory — commutes work)
- Belief that journaling "works" (the prompts are useful as a thinking tool whether or not you buy the wider story about gratitude changing your life)

The [daily ritual that sticks](/blog/daily-ritual-that-sticks) post covers the habit-design question in more depth — specifically why rituals collapse at week two and how to design them so they don't. The five-minute journal survives when it's short enough to pick up on a tired Thursday. Five minutes is designed to clear that bar.
