Athletes don't tend to call it affirmations. They call it self-talk — and sports psychologists have studied it rigorously since the 1980s.

A 2011 meta-analysis by Hatzigeorgiadis, Zourbanos, Galanis, and Theodorakis, published in *Perspectives on Psychological Science*, reviewed 32 studies and found a moderate positive effect of self-talk on athletic performance (effect size .48). Not marginal. Not limited to elite athletes. Consistent across a range of sports and skill levels.

The useful question isn't whether it works. It's which kind works.

## Instructional vs. motivational

Sports psychologists draw a clean line between two types of self-talk.

**Instructional** self-talk directs technique: "keep your elbow up," "drive from your hips," "light feet." It's most effective for tasks requiring precision — a tennis serve, a golf swing, a free throw. It keeps the body coordinated when nerves push you to rush.

**Motivational** self-talk manages effort and emotion: "I've done this before," "stay focused," "one point at a time." It's more useful for high-intensity or high-stakes moments — a sprint finish, a set point, a close game in the final minutes.

Both outperform silence. The Hatzigeorgiadis research suggests instructional cues work better for fine-motor tasks, motivational cues for endurance and effort. In practice, most athletes use both without labeling them.

## The problem with generic cheerleading

"I am an elite athlete." "I am unstoppable." The generic content you'll find on sports motivation accounts tends to be what psychologists call overassertive — it states something you can't fully believe in the moment you most need it.

If you just dropped a pass in the end zone, telling yourself "I am the most confident receiver on this team" is a difficult sell. Your brain notices the gap between the claim and the recent evidence, and the dissonance can make things worse, not better.

What holds up under pressure is specific and honest: "I've caught this ball in practice a thousand times. I know how to do this." Not a declaration — a reminder. [The same principle applies in everyday contexts](/blog/affirmations-that-dont-feel-fake): the phrases that stick are rooted in something real, not something aspirational.

## Short cues, not monologues

The most practical self-talk for athletes is compressed. A keyword or short phrase, not a paragraph.

"Smooth." "Attack." "Stay." "Mine." Single words that cue a known physical state. These are rehearsed in training until they reliably pull the athlete back to the feeling they want in competition. The cue isn't magic — it's an anchor to a kinesthetic memory.

This is close to what [attention-based practices do more broadly](/blog/attention-as-manifestation): a specific, repeatable prompt that redirects focus toward what you're trying to do, not away from what you're worried about.

## After the mistake

The most important self-talk moment isn't the warm-up. It's the thirty seconds after something goes wrong.

Research on athletic performance and recovery consistently shows that self-critical spirals after mistakes are among the most reliable predictors of continued errors. The athlete who drops the ball and then replays dropping it is less likely to catch the next one — not because of confidence, but because attention is finite, and they've spent it on the wrong thing.

Useful self-talk after a mistake is short and forward-facing: "Next play." "Reset." "Move." Not absolution, not self-flagellation — just a redirect. [Visualization research supports this](/blog/does-visualization-actually-work): briefly imagining the correct execution rather than replaying the error restores neural readiness faster than dwelling.

## Building a practice

If you want to develop a self-talk practice:

1. Choose one instructional cue for a skill you're currently working on. Make it exactly what you'd say to yourself in practice when it's going well.
2. Choose one motivational cue for your hardest moments. It should be true, not aspirational.
3. Add a reset phrase for after mistakes. Three words or fewer.

Use them consistently in practice before relying on them in competition. The phrase needs to be rehearsed to become a reflex.

Most athletes never run out of things to say to themselves. The question is whether those things are helping or not.
