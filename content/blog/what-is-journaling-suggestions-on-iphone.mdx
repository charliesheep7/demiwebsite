You've probably seen it: a notification from your iPhone suggesting you write about your morning walk, or the coffee you apparently had at a place you forgot you visited. Journaling Suggestions is Apple's attempt to solve the hardest part of building a journaling habit — the blank page.

Whether it succeeds depends on what you're trying to do with a journal.

## What Journaling Suggestions actually does

Introduced with iOS 17 and the Journal app, Journaling Suggestions uses on-device data to surface "memorable moments" from your recent days. It draws from several sources:

- **Photos** — clusters of images from a specific time or place
- **Health and Fitness** — workouts you completed, sleep data, mindfulness sessions
- **Music and Podcasts** — what you listened to
- **Location** — places you visited
- **Contacts** — people you connected with
- **Calendar** — events you attended

The processing is on-device, which means Apple isn't reading this data on its servers — it's your phone doing pattern recognition locally and generating prompts from what it finds. You can control which categories feed into suggestions under Settings → Privacy & Security → Journaling Suggestions.

Third-party journaling apps — including Day One, which added support for the feature — can also pull from Journaling Suggestions through an Apple API.

The practical result: instead of opening a blank journal and trying to remember what happened yesterday, you get a photo from Tuesday's walk, a line noting you ran 4.2 km, a card showing you called your sister. Specific starting points instead of a blank stare.

## What it solves (and doesn't)

The blank-page problem is real. Research on journaling consistently finds that the biggest barrier isn't motivation — it's the cognitive overhead of deciding what to write about before you've started. A specific prompt dramatically lowers that overhead.

Journaling Suggestions is essentially an automated version of [a guided journal](/blog/guided-journal): someone has done the "what should I write about today?" work for you. For people who want to journal but keep abandoning it after three days, this is a real affordance.

What it doesn't solve is the depth question. A suggestion that says "You were at Washington Square Park at 3 PM" gives you a topic. It doesn't give you a reason to reflect on what was happening internally at 3 PM — whether you were anxious, or at peace, or replaying a conversation. The feature is prompting memory retrieval, not self-examination.

Expressive writing research, going back to James Pennebaker's work at UT Austin, finds that the journaling that reliably affects well-being is not logging ("went to the park") but processing ("went to the park, still turning over what he said, and I think I'm angrier than I let myself feel in the moment"). The suggestion gets you to the park. The writing is still yours to do.

## How to make it useful

The most effective approach treats a Journaling Suggestion as a door, not an assignment.

When a suggestion comes up — a photo from Wednesday, a workout you finished — use it as a starting point, but let your actual writing go where it wants to go. You don't have to write about the park. You have to write about Wednesday. The park is just the way in.

A concrete approach:
1. Open the suggestion.
2. Ask yourself one question before writing: *What was actually going on that day?*
3. Write toward whatever surfaces — not a description of the photo, but whatever association it triggers.
4. Aim for three to five minutes. Enough to get below the surface. Not so long the habit requires a special morning.

This is essentially what [morning journal prompts](/blog/morning-journal-prompts) do in a more structured way — use a question to get below the "I had coffee" layer into what's actually moving through you.

## On privacy, practically

The on-device processing means your journal content doesn't leave your phone when suggestions are generated. What does go to Apple's servers: the Journal app's backups through iCloud, like any other app, if iCloud backup is enabled. That's the actual privacy consideration — not the suggestions feature itself, but standard iCloud behavior.

If you're writing things you'd want encrypted, use a journaling app with end-to-end encryption by default (Day One's paid tier does this; standard iCloud backup does not offer the same).

## The habit underneath the feature

Journaling Suggestions won't build a journaling habit by itself. What it does is reduce one specific friction point — choosing a topic — that stops a lot of people before they start.

The research on [daily rituals that stick](/blog/daily-ritual-that-sticks) points to the same principle: reduce friction, attach to something you're already doing, keep the bar low enough that a normal Tuesday doesn't end it. A five-minute morning journal off a Journaling Suggestion fits all three.

The goal isn't a perfect journal. It's five minutes of sitting with your own experience instead of narrating someone else's on a feed.
