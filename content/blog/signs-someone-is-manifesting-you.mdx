You keep thinking about them. They text the morning you'd been replaying a conversation. You heard their name three times this week.

Something's happening. Whether it's them or it's you is worth understanding — and the answer changes what you do with it.

## What people say the signs are

Across TikTok, Reddit, and manifestation forums, the same signs appear again and again:

- Intrusive thoughts of someone you haven't spoken to recently
- Vivid dreams that feel more real than usual
- Synchronicities — their name in random places, songs that remind you of them, numbers you associate with them
- A sudden sense of them being present, or a physical sensation (the hiccup thing is weirdly consistent across traditions)
- They reach out unexpectedly, or you hear about them from someone else

These are genuinely common experiences. The question is what they mean.

## What's actually happening

The honest version: you can't tell if someone else is manifesting you. What you can observe is your own attention — and your own attention is doing something real here.

Your brain has a filter called the [reticular activating system](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Reticular_activating_system). It surfaces what you've flagged as relevant and suppresses what you haven't. When someone occupies your mind — consciously or not — your RAS starts pulling their name, their face, and adjacent associations into your awareness from noise you'd normally screen out.

This is the same mechanism behind [how attention works in manifestation generally](/blog/attention-as-manifestation): what you hold in view becomes more visible. The question isn't whether someone is sending you their thoughts across a room. The question is what your attention is doing, and what it's telling you.

That said: [confirmation bias](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Confirmation_bias) is also doing real work here. You're seeing them everywhere partly because you're primed to notice it. That's not magic — it's just how memory and pattern recognition work.

## Why "are they manifesting me?" is the wrong question

When you wonder whether someone is manifesting you, the real question underneath is usually: *do they think about me the way I think about them?*

That's a genuine question about connection and reciprocity, and it deserves a genuine answer — not a list of signs.

The problem with the signs framing is that it puts you in a passive position, waiting to read evidence from the outside. It also gives you no actionable information even if the signs line up. If all thirteen signs are present, then what? You still have to decide what to do.

The useful version of the question is: *what am I hoping for here, and am I doing anything about it?* That's a question you can actually answer.

## The one thing these signs are genuinely useful for

When you notice you keep thinking about someone, or that their name keeps surfacing, it's worth treating that as data — not about them, but about you.

The intrusive-thought pattern is your own attention pointing at something unresolved. Maybe it's a relationship you want to revisit. Maybe it's a conversation you didn't finish. Maybe it's [a future version of your life you're quietly building toward](/blog/manifesting-a-specific-person) and this person figures into it.

You don't need to decide whether they're manifesting you. You need to ask what the attention means on your end, and whether you're being honest with yourself about it.

Take thirty seconds with that question. Not half a day of reassurance-seeking. Thirty seconds of placing your actual attention on what's really there.

## What to do if you believe in this

If you're someone who does hold a spiritual framework — who believes attention can reach across distance — then the signs matter less than what you do with them.

The consistent advice across manifestation traditions is the same: instead of watching for signs from the outside, [focus your own attention on what you actually want](/blog/does-manifestation-actually-work). The signs are a byproduct of attention; attention itself is the practice.

Both the believer and the skeptic end up at the same place: do something with what you're noticing, rather than cataloging evidence.
