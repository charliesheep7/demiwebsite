Every January, the same notebook. The same optimistic first page. Sometimes the second. By February it's on the nightstand holding a glass of water.

The prompts aren't the problem. The prompts are usually fine. The problem is that most new year journaling frameworks ask you to answer twenty-five questions about your "best self," "core values," and "non-negotiables" in one sitting — and by question six you're either asleep or writing something you don't actually believe.

Here's a shorter list. Seven prompts. Pick three. Write badly.

## What the year actually looked like

Most new year reflection starts with "what went well?" — which is fine, but misses the more useful question underneath it.

**Try this instead:**

_What kept happening that I kept pretending wasn't happening?_

This is the prompt that takes you somewhere. Not the highlight reel. The pattern you noticed in October, then December, and quietly filed under "next year." Write that down first.

## The prompts

These are designed to be used one at a time, not in a single two-hour session. Pick the one that annoys you most. That's usually the one worth doing.

**1. What did I keep starting and stopping this year?**

Not to shame yourself — to notice the pattern. The gym membership, the meditation app, the journal itself. Something in the friction is information. What was the friction, specifically?

**2. What did I want that I never said out loud?**

Not the goal you told people about. The one you kept private because it felt too big, too unlikely, or somehow embarrassing. Write it down now. You don't have to show anyone.

**3. What did I stop wanting?**

The job title, the relationship shape, the version of yourself you were aiming at three years ago. Some of those have quietly expired. Noticing what no longer fits is useful — you stop spending attention on it.

**4. Where did my attention actually go?**

Not where you planned to put it. Where did it go? The commute, the inbox, the news, the worry about one specific thing that turned out to be fine. Your attention is a limited resource. Noticing where it leaks is the first step to spending it more deliberately.

**5. What one decision, if I'd made it differently, would have changed the year?**

This isn't regret. It's calibration. What does the answer tell you about what you actually value? That's the thing worth carrying forward.

**6. What kind of Tuesday did I want?**

Not the exceptional days — the average ones. If the life you want is working, what does a regular Tuesday look like? Not the vacation. The week in between vacations. Being specific here is the whole point. "More peace" is not specific. "Not checking my phone before I've had coffee" is.

**7. What am I willing to keep doing imperfectly?**

The habits that survived — even badly — are more useful data than the ones you planned to start. Something you do three times a week with low enthusiasm beats something you do perfectly once and abandon. What are you already willing to show up for, even when you don't feel like it?

## How to actually use these

One prompt at a time. Fifteen minutes. Write in complete sentences, even badly-constructed ones — the prose forces you to say something specific in a way that bullet points don't.

If you've tried [manifestation journaling](/blog/affirmation-journal) before and it felt hollow, the issue was probably the prompts asking you to affirm things you don't believe yet. These prompts go in the other direction — backward into the year you actually had, not forward into a self you're performing.

You don't need to do all seven. If you do all seven in one sitting, you'll probably write shallow answers to most of them. One prompt, real time, honest writing beats seven prompts and a glass of water on the nightstand.

For a concrete example of what a real reflective entry looks like — including what to do when you write three sentences and stop — [this walkthrough helps](/blog/reflective-journal-example).

## Why writing it down matters

There's a [study published in the *Journal of Personality and Social Psychology*](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC12085957/) showing that specificity and written commitment are the two factors that most reliably predict whether someone follows through on a goal. Not motivation. Not belief. Specificity and commitment, written down.

The mechanism isn't mystical. When you write something specific — "I want to leave my job by July" rather than "I want a new job" — you activate what neuroscientists call the reticular activating system: the part of your brain that filters incoming information for what's relevant. Write down the specific thing and your brain starts flagging evidence for it. The job listing in your inbox. The conversation at a dinner party. The newsletter you almost deleted.

This is also why [scripting](/blog/scripting-manifestation) works for some people — not because of any metaphysical mechanism, but because writing in specific detail is a reliable way to load something into your attention.

None of this requires belief. It requires writing something specific down.

## What this isn't

This isn't a new year, new you situation. The prompts don't produce transformation. They produce information — about where your attention actually went, what you actually want, what you're actually willing to do.

That information is more useful than a resolution. Resolutions tell you what you wish were true. These prompts tell you what's already true, which is the only thing worth building on.

If the journal feels like too much — if even fifteen minutes is too long, if the blank page reliably stops you — that's a real signal. Some people's version of year-end reflection is shorter. [Thirty seconds of attention on what you actually want](/blog/30-second-daily-ritual) is not a joke. It's the minimum viable version of the same practice.

If [vision boards and fresh-start rituals](/blog/vision-boards-for-new-years) are more your speed, those work too. The medium matters less than the regularity.

Pick the format that actually survives into February.
